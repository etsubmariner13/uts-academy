**Getting to Know the Terms**

* **Sentence Posture Topologies**: The physical geometric shapes that different kinds of sentences take on grid paper. Instead of thinking of grammar as abstract rules in a textbook, grammar is the actual mechanical posture (nested, open, directed, or crowded) of the continuous wire.

* **Declarative Equilibrium**: A statement of verified fact drawn as balanced, concentric, or smoothly nested loops sitting in resting physical balance.

* **Interrogative Shear**: A question drawn as an open, offset loop that leaves an un-closed spatial gap on the page, creating physical mechanical tension that demands an answer loop to restore balance.

* **Imperative Tension Vectors**: A command or directive drawn as a straight, directed line pointing kinetic force directly at an adjacent idea loop.

* **Exclamatory Overlap Clusters**: An urgent or high-energy statement drawn as a dense, high-compaction cluster of overlapping loops that rapidly claims local spatial room.

* **Polysemic Channels**: The shared physical intersections where two or more idea loops cross, revealing hidden connections, shared mechanical loads, and mutual dependencies between separate clauses.

**The Idea in Plain English**

In Module 2.1, you learned how to use the Planar Drawing Engine: laying down your outer boundary circle (C₀), sizing major ideas larger than supporting details, and using dashed lines to show when one idea passes underneath another.

**Now, in Module 2.2, we explore how different types of sentences physically change the shape of the wire.**

Think of a person standing on a firm floor.

* When they stand balanced on both feet, they are in a Declarative posture: stable, quiet, and at rest.

* When they lean forward onto one foot with an arm reaching out, they are in an Interrogative posture: off-balance, open-ended, and actively searching for something to lean on.

* When they firmly push a heavy cart forward, they are in an Imperative posture: directing physical force along a straight line.

* When they jump and brace for impact, they are in an Exclamatory posture: tightly bunching their muscles into a dense cluster.

Language functions in the exact same physical way.

Over 2,300 years ago in Athens, Aristotle compiled the Organon, showing that every spoken sentence is an arrangement of real categories: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, and affection. Aristotle recognized that a question is an incomplete relation seeking a predicate, while an assertion is a completed connection that affirms or denies a real state of affairs.

In the Unified Tensile System, we do not treat these categories as abstract grammar terms. We draw them as real physical postures on grid paper:

* A **Declarative** statement (e.g., "The foundation rests on granite") closes its loop smoothly, resting in stable equilibrium.

* An **Interrogative** question (e.g., "Where is the load shifting?") draws an open, off-center loop that creates localized mechanical shear, physically showing that the thought is incomplete until an answer loop locks it shut.

* An **Imperative** command (e.g., "Tighten the anchor bolt") draws an arrow of line tension pressing directly into a target node.

* An **Exclamatory** alert (e.g., "The pressure valve is blowing!") draws a tight cluster of overlapping loops that quickly claims local spatial room.

When these different shapes cross each other on the grid, they form **Polysemic Channels**. These intersection points are not drawing accidents; they are literal structural joints where separate ideas share the same physical coordinates. By marking these junctions with small Fold-Circles, you instantly see how a question pulls on a fact, or how a command alters an entire system.

**Step-by-Step Drawing Practice**

* **Step 1**: Place a fresh sheet of standard 5.0 mm metric grid paper (or a US Quad-ruled pad) flat on your desk and take your pencil.

* **Step 2**: **Establishing Your Outer Workspace (C₀)**: Draw one large, smooth, continuous outer circle (C₀) filling roughly 80% of your sheet, closing cleanly where your pencil started. This sets your total spatial clearance budget.

* **Step 3**: **Drawing the Declarative Baseline Loop (C₁)**: Inside C₀ near the top-left, draw a solid, perfectly closed circular loop (C₁) to represent a stable, factual statement (for example: "The bridge span is 100 meters"). Make C₁ large and dominant, bringing its outer edge to touch the inner perimeter of C₀ at a single point.

* **Step 4**: **Drawing the Interrogative Shear Loop (C₂)**:

  - Directly adjacent to C₁, draw a secondary loop (C₂) representing an open question (for example: "Can the cables carry the winter ice load?").

  - Do not close C₂ into a full circle; leave a 2-grid-unit gap in its perimeter facing outward, and offset its center by 2 grid squares relative to C₁.

  - Bring the drawn side of C₂ to cross C₁ at two distinct points. Notice how the open gap visually and physically demands closure.

* **Step 5**: **Drawing the Imperative Tension Vector (C₃)**:

  - From the open gap of C₂, draw a straight, bold directional line vector (C₃) pointing straight toward the center-right of your page (representing an actionable directive, such as: "Inspect cable strain gauge #4").

  - Terminate the line at a dedicated target circle (C₄) nested in the lower-right quadrant.

* **Step 6**: **Drawing the Exclamatory Cluster Node (C₅)**: Inside C₄, draw two small, tightly overlapping loops (C₅ and C₆) to represent an urgent condition (e.g., "Tension spike detected!"). Notice how the overlapping lines increase the line density in that corner of the page.

* **Step 7**: **Centering 1-Unit Cardinal Fold-Circles**:

  - Locate every point on the sheet where loops touch, cross, or where vectors attach to perimeters.

  - Center your pencil on each exact intersection coordinate and draw a small 1-unit circle extending exactly 1 grid square Up, Down, Left, and Right.

  - Count your Fold-Circles. Look at the completed drawing: you have mapped a complete operational story (Fact ──► Question ──► Directive ──► Alert) as an interconnected mechanical network of physical postures.
